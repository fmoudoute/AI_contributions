Introduction

This is the fourth article in my Making of RAG series. So far, we have prepared our text with chunking and tokenization, and in Part 3 we turned every chunk into a vector with embeddings. Our building has its foundation, its materials and its blueprint.

Now someone walks into the building with a question. Somebody has to know, among thousands of rooms, which ones hold the answer, and has to find them in a few milliseconds. That somebody is retrieval, the concierge of our building (yes, another metaphor... I promise this is the last one for this article :).

We will do all three, still on the same Luckin Coffee Q1 2026 earnings transcript. But this time, I want to go one step further than "look, it works on my example". We will measure it.

Retrieval, or finding the needle

Retrieval is the step that takes the user's question and returns the K chunks (usually 3 to 10) that will be given to the LLM as context. It sounds simple, and in Part 3 we already did a version of it with a single dot product. But retrieval is where a RAG system succeeds or fails, and it has the same impacts I used in the previous articles:

1 - Accuracy

The LLM can only answer from what it receives. If the right chunk is not in the top K, the model will either say "I don't know" (the good case) or answer confidently from the wrong chunk (the bad case). In my experience, when people say "the LLM hallucinated", the cause very often turns out to be a retrieval problem.

2 - Cost

Every chunk we retrieve is pasted into the prompt, and as we saw in Part 2, every token is paid for. Retrieving 10 chunks of 128 tokens adds about 1,300 tokens to every question. Better retrieval means we can afford a smaller K, and a cheaper prompt.

3 - Process Efficiency

Retrieval runs while the user is waiting. Comparing a question with 107 chunks is instant; comparing it with 10 million chunks is not. The way we search (exactly, or approximately) decides the latency of the whole system.

Before anything: a test set

You can't improve what you don't measure. In Part 3, I tested semantic search with three questions that I had chosen myself, and I could see that it worked. That's a demo, not a measurement. So before comparing retrieval methods, I built a small test set: 12 questions about the transcript, each with a short phrase that the right chunk must contain.

I mixed two kinds of questions on purpose. Some use words that are not in the transcript ("shops", "people work for"), and some use exact terms, acronyms and numbers ("GMV", "S&M", "non-GAAP").

For each question, we look at the rank of the right chunk in the results, and we compute three classic metrics:

  • Hit@1: how many times the right chunk is ranked first.
  • Hit@3: how many times it is in the top 3 (if we give 3 chunks to the LLM, this is what matters).
  • MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank): the average of 1/rank. Rank 1 gives 1.0, rank 2 gives 0.5, rank 4 gives 0.25. An MRR of 1.0 means "always first".

Twelve questions is small, and I'll come back to that. But it's already enough to see real differences, and it's infinitely better than zero.

Chunking, the Part 3 way

In Part 3, we saw that one of our 120-word chunks was 307 tokens long because of the Chinese paragraphs, and that the embedding model silently ignored everything after token 256. So this time I took my own advice and chunked in the tokens of the embedding model: 128 tokens per chunk, with 32 tokens of overlap.

def token_chunk(text, tokenizer, chunk_tokens=128, overlap=32):
    tokenizer.no_truncation()
    enc = tokenizer.encode(text, add_special_tokens=False)
    tokenizer.enable_truncation(max_length=256)
    step = chunk_tokens - overlap
    chunks = []
    for start in range(0, len(enc.offsets), step):
        window = enc.offsets[start:start + chunk_tokens]
        chunks.append(text[window[0][0]:window[-1][1]])
    return chunks

The trick is in enc.offsets: for every token, the tokenizer tells us where it starts and ends in the original text, so we can cut the text exactly on token boundaries. The result: 107 chunks, and none goes over 130 tokens (128 plus the two special tokens). Nothing is truncated anymore.

Keyword search, done right: BM25

In Part 3, our keyword search simply counted the words in common between the question and the chunk, and it got fooled by words like "the" and "company". The fix has existed for decades, and it's still used everywhere today (Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Lucene...): BM25.

BM25 improves on word counting with three ideas:

  1. Rare words matter more (IDF). A word that appears in every chunk ("the") says nothing; a word that appears in one chunk ("Yunnan") says a lot.
  2. Repetition has diminishing returns (k1). A chunk that says "revenue" 10 times is not 10 times more relevant than a chunk that says it once.
  3. Long chunks are penalized (b). A long chunk has more chances to contain any word, so its score is normalized by its length.

Here it is from scratch, in about 25 lines:

import math
import re
from collections import Counter


def tokenize(text):
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9&$.,]+", text.lower().replace("?", ""))


class BM25:
    def __init__(self, docs, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
        self.k1, self.b = k1, b
        self.docs = [Counter(tokenize(d)) for d in docs]
        self.lengths = np.array([sum(d.values()) for d in self.docs])
        self.avg_len = self.lengths.mean()
        n = len(docs)
        df = Counter(w for d in self.docs for w in d)
        self.idf = {w: math.log(1 + (n - f + 0.5) / (f + 0.5)) for w, f in df.items()}

    def scores(self, query):
        out = np.zeros(len(self.docs))
        for w in set(tokenize(query)):
            if w not in self.idf:
                continue
            for i, d in enumerate(self.docs):
                tf = d[w]
                if tf:
                    norm = self.k1 * (1 - self.b + self.b * self.lengths[i] / self.avg_len)
                    out[i] += self.idf[w] * tf * (self.k1 + 1) / (tf + norm)
        return out

Note that my tokenize keeps characters like &, $, . and ,, so that "S&M", "$300" or "33,596" stay in one piece. As we saw in Part 2, the way you tokenize decides what you can find!

And for the semantic side, nothing new: it's exactly the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model and the dot product from Part 3.

chunk_vectors = embedder.embed(chunks)

    return chunk_vectors @ embedder.embed([query])[0]

Where BM25 fails. "How many shops does the company operate?" puts the right chunk at rank 17. Let's look at the weights BM25 gives to each word of the question:

BM25 weights (IDF) for: How many shops does the company operate?
   how=4.28, many=0.00, shops=0.00, does=0.00, the=0.48, company=3.43, operate=0.00
   BM25 top chunk: ...to know whether the company will consider adapting other shareholder reward initiatives su...

This is the funniest result of the article. "Shops", "many", "does" and "operate" never appear in the transcript, so they score zero. And "how" appears only once in the whole transcript, which makes it, according to IDF, one of the "rarest and most meaningful" words of the question! BM25 ended up returning an analyst's question about dividends, because it contains "how" and "company". IDF is a great idea on a large corpus; on 107 chunks, it's easy to fool.

Where semantic search fails. "How many people work for Luckin?" ranks the right chunk 4th. The answer is in a sentence about "our 190,000 Luckin team members", and the top 3 were the introduction chunks of the call, full of "Luckin Coffee". The word "Luckin" dominated the vector, and the meaning of "people work for" ≈ "team members" was not strong enough. BM25 ranked it 2nd, but mostly by luck: only "for" and "Luckin" matched, and the chunk it ranked 1st matched exactly the same two words.

So each method sees something the other misses. Can we get the best of both?

Round 2: hybrid search with Reciprocal Rank Fusion

The obvious idea would be to add the two scores. But the scores don't live on the same scale: a BM25 score of 5.2 and a cosine similarity of 0.48 can't be compared. The elegant solution is to ignore the scores and only keep the ranks. That's Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), introduced by Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher in 2009:

RRF(chunk) = Σ 1 / (k + rank of the chunk in each result list), with k = 60 by convention.

A chunk ranked first by both methods gets the highest score; a chunk ranked first by one method and 50th by the other still gets a decent score. And the code is tiny:

def rrf(*score_lists, k=60):
    fused = np.zeros(len(chunks))
    for scores in score_lists:
        ranks = np.argsort(np.argsort(-scores))  # 0 = best
        fused += 1 / (k + ranks + 1)
    return fused


hybrid_scores = rrf(bm25.scores(query), semantic_scores(query))

(The double argsort is a small numpy trick: the first one sorts the chunks, the second one gives the rank of each chunk.)

After:

BM25 (keyword)         Hit@1 =  6/12   Hit@3 =  9/12   MRR = 0.66
Semantic               Hit@1 =  7/12   Hit@3 = 10/12   MRR = 0.73
Hybrid (RRF)           Hit@1 =  9/12   Hit@3 = 12/12   MRR = 0.86

12 out of 12 questions have the right chunk in the top 3. Neither method could do that alone. "How many people work for Luckin?" jumps from rank 4 (semantic) and rank 2 (BM25) to rank 1, and "How many shops?" goes from rank 17 (BM25) to rank 2. This is why hybrid search has become the default in most production RAG systems I have seen, and why most vector databases now offer it out of the box.

Round 3: re-ranking, or why the top result is not always the best one

There is one more limit, and it comes from how embeddings work. In Part 3, the question and the chunk were embedded separately, each one into its own vector, and then compared. This type of model is called a bi-encoder. It's fast (chunk vectors are computed once, in advance), but the model never sees the question and the chunk together. Everything the chunk says has to be squeezed into one vector before anyone knows what the question will be, so small but important details (is it the China store count or the total store count?) can get lost.

A cross-encoder reads the question and the chunk together, in a single input, and outputs one relevance score. It's much more accurate, but much slower: it must run the full model once per (question, chunk) pair, at query time, and nothing can be computed in advance. Running it on millions of chunks is out of the question.

So we use both, in two stages:

  1. Retrieve a few candidates fast (here, the top 20 of the hybrid search).
  2. Re-rank only those 20 with the cross-encoder.
class CrossEncoder:
    def __init__(self, model_id="cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2"):
        self.tokenizer = Tokenizer.from_file(hf_hub_download(model_id, "tokenizer.json"))
        self.tokenizer.enable_truncation(max_length=512)
        self.tokenizer.enable_padding()
        self.session = ort.InferenceSession(hf_hub_download(model_id, "onnx/model.onnx"))

    def score(self, query, docs):
        # The query and the chunk are read TOGETHER, in one input
        enc = self.tokenizer.encode_batch([(query, d) for d in docs])
        feeds = {
            "input_ids": np.array([e.ids for e in enc], dtype=np.int64),
            "attention_mask": np.array([e.attention_mask for e in enc], dtype=np.int64),
            "token_type_ids": np.array([e.type_ids for e in enc], dtype=np.int64),
        }
        return self.session.run(None, feeds)[0][:, 0]


reranker = CrossEncoder()


def rerank(query, candidate_scores, top_n=20):
    candidates = np.argsort(-candidate_scores)[:top_n]
    new_scores = np.full(len(chunks), -np.inf)
    new_scores[candidates] = reranker.score(query, [chunks[i] for i in candidates])
    return new_scores

Notice the (query, d) pair given to the tokenizer: this is what makes it a cross-encoder. The tokenizer builds a single input [CLS] question [SEP] chunk [SEP], and the type_ids tell the model which part is which.

After:

BM25 (keyword)         Hit@1 =  6/12   Hit@3 =  9/12   MRR = 0.66
Semantic               Hit@1 =  7/12   Hit@3 = 10/12   MRR = 0.73
Hybrid (RRF)           Hit@1 =  9/12   Hit@3 = 12/12   MRR = 0.86
Hybrid + re-rank       Hit@1 = 10/12   Hit@3 = 11/12   MRR = 0.89

The re-ranker improves Hit@1 (10 out of 12) and the MRR... but Hit@3 went down from 12 to 11. One question got worse: "How many shops does the company operate?" went from rank 2 to rank 5. So I looked at what the re-ranker put first:

   Re-ranker top chunk: ...In China, we added 2,531 net new stores during the quarter, bringing our total store count to 3...

The sentence continues with "...to 33,419". And honestly? That is a correct answer. 33,419 is the number of stores in China, and 33,596 is the total including the 177 overseas stores. The question "how many shops does the company operate?" is ambiguous, and my test set only accepted one of the two answers. The re-ranker wasn't wrong; my test set was too strict.

I decided to leave this result as it is in the article, because it teaches two lessons I find more valuable than a perfect score:

  • A re-ranker is not magic. It usually improves the top of the ranking, but it can move things in ways you don't expect, so measure it on your own data before adding it (it also adds latency at every query).
  • Your evaluation is only as good as your test set. Before blaming the model, look at the failures one by one. Sometimes the model is right and the "expected answer" is wrong. In a real project, I would accept both chunks for this question, and add more questions: 12 is enough to see trends, not to make a final decision.

Retrieval at scale: exact vs. approximate search

So far, we compared each question with all 107 chunks. This is called exact (or brute-force) search, and for 107 chunks it's instant. But what happens with millions of chunks?

To test this, I needed a much bigger knowledge base than one transcript. So I generated 200,000 synthetic vectors of 384 dimensions (like all-MiniLM-L6-v2), grouped into 2,000 "topics" with a lot of noise, to imitate real texts that talk about the same subjects in different ways. Let me be clear: these are not real embeddings, and the timings depend on my laptop. But the trade-off they show is real.

Exact search

def exact_search(q, k=10):
    scores = vectors @ q
    top = np.argpartition(-scores, k)[:k]
    return top[np.argsort(-scores[top])]

Approximate search: IVF from scratch

The idea of an IVF index (Inverted File index) is simple. Before any question arrives, we group the vectors into clusters with k-means. When a question arrives, we first find the few clusters closest to it, and we only search inside those clusters. It's like a library: you don't read every book, you go to the right shelf first.

def kmeans(data, n_clusters, iterations=10):
    centroids = data[rng.choice(len(data), n_clusters, replace=False)]
    for _ in range(iterations):
        assignment = np.argmax(data @ centroids.T, axis=1)  # closest centroid (cosine)
        for c in range(n_clusters):
            members = data[assignment == c]
            if len(members):
                centroid = members.mean(axis=0)
                centroids[c] = centroid / np.linalg.norm(centroid)
    return centroids


N_LISTS = 450  # rule of thumb: about sqrt(N)
sample = vectors[rng.choice(N, 50_000, replace=False)]
centroids = kmeans(sample.copy(), N_LISTS)
assignment = np.concatenate([np.argmax(chunk @ centroids.T, axis=1)
                             for chunk in np.array_split(vectors, 20)])
# Store each cluster's vectors next to each other in memory (like a real vector DB)
order = np.argsort(assignment, kind="stable")
sorted_vectors = vectors[order]
bounds = np.searchsorted(assignment[order], np.arange(N_LISTS + 1))


def ivf_search(q, k=10, n_probe=5):
    closest_clusters = np.argsort(-(centroids @ q))[:n_probe]
    candidates = np.concatenate([np.arange(bounds[c], bounds[c + 1]) for c in closest_clusters])
    scores = np.concatenate([sorted_vectors[bounds[c]:bounds[c + 1]] @ q for c in closest_clusters])
    top = np.argsort(-scores)[:k]
    return order[candidates[top]], len(candidates)

The n_probe parameter is the number of clusters we visit. To measure the quality, we compare the 10 results of IVF with the 10 results of the exact search: that's the recall@10 (10 out of 10 = 100%).

After:

Knowledge base: 200,000 vectors x 384 dims = 307 MB

Exact search :   5.90 ms per query, scanned 200,000 vectors
IVF index built in 2.6 s (450 clusters)

IVF n_probe= 1:   0.14 ms per query, scanned      468 vectors, recall@10 = 89%
IVF n_probe= 5:   0.43 ms per query, scanned    2,283 vectors, recall@10 = 92%
IVF n_probe=20:   1.51 ms per query, scanned    8,978 vectors, recall@10 = 95%
IVF n_probe=50:   3.72 ms per query, scanned   22,361 vectors, recall@10 = 97%

This table is, for me, the essence of approximate search. With n_probe=1, we scan 0.2% of the vectors and search about 40 times faster, but we lose 11% of the true top-10 results. With n_probe=50, we get 97% of them back, but the speed-up is almost gone. There is no free lunch: approximate search trades a bit of accuracy for a lot of speed, and n_probe is the knob that sets the balance.

At 200,000 vectors, exact search at 6 ms is still perfectly fine, and that is an important point: you don't always need approximate search. For a few hundred thousand chunks, a brute-force search (or pgvector without an index) is simpler and exact. Approximate search becomes necessary in the millions, where exact search grows linearly with the number of chunks.

In production, vector databases like Qdrant, Milvus, Weaviate, Pinecone or pgvector implement these ideas in optimized code, and many of them rely on another family of indexes called HNSW (a graph where each vector is connected to its neighbours, and the search "walks" toward the question). Different algorithm, same trade-off, and the same kind of knob (ef_search instead of n_probe).

Practical considerations

  1. Build a test set first. Even 20 to 50 questions with their expected chunks will tell you more than any leaderboard. Re-run it every time you change the chunking, the embedding model or the retrieval method.
  2. Start with hybrid search. BM25 catches exact terms (product names, acronyms, numbers, IDs); embeddings catch meaning. RRF combines them without any tuning.
  3. Add a re-ranker if your top-1 matters, and measure it. It adds latency at every query (here, 20 extra model calls per question), so retrieve a reasonable number of candidates (20 to 50) and re-rank only those.
  4. Choose K with the cost in mind. Every retrieved chunk is paid for in the prompt. If your Hit@3 is already high, giving 10 chunks to the LLM mostly adds cost and noise.
  5. Don't rely on a fixed similarity threshold. As we saw in Part 3, a good match can have a cosine of 0.48. Scores depend on the model and on the length of the texts; ranks are much more robust.
  6. Exact first, approximate when you need it. Below a few hundred thousand chunks, exact search is simple and fast enough. Above that, use the approximate index of your vector database, and tune its knob with your test set.

Conclusion

Retrieval is the moment where all the previous steps meet. The chunking decides what can be found, the tokenization decides what each method can see, and the embeddings decide what "similar" means. And as we saw, no single method wins everywhere: BM25 was fooled by the word "how", the embedding model by the word "Luckin", and the re-ranker "failed" on a question where it was actually right.

What I take from this experiment is that retrieval is less about choosing the best algorithm, and more about combining complementary methods and measuring them honestly on your own data. Going from 9 to 12 questions in the top 3 didn't require a bigger model, only a better combination of two simple ones.
